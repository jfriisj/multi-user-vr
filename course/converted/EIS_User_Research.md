4 User Research Process
4.1 Problem Hypothesis
We hypothesize that novice clinical staff and trainees experience coordination failures in acute, multi-person events
primarily because of:
1. Ambiguous role and ownership during overlapping tasks; and
2. Lack of short, pragmatic decision aids for quickly evolving complications; and
3. Low perceived fidelity of current simulation training which limits stress-exposure and adaptive practice.
These factors reduce decision speed and confidence in time-critical moments (e.g., trauma calls, unexpected intra-
operative bleeding) and increase dependency on senior staff rather than reliable team processes.
Primary success criteria (how we will know the hypothesis is true):
• Faster and more consistent time-to-priority decisions in pilot simulations (median reduction expected).
• Improved trainee self-rated confidence (pre/post Likert increase) when left to perform initial actions.
• Fewer coordination conflicts (measured as count of conflicting orders or role-claims) during micro-simulations.

4.7 Prioritized problems
Ranked by cross-interview frequency and safety impact:
1. Role and ownership ambiguity during multi-team events (high frequency for trainees in trauma/operating set-
tings) — Jacob explicitly called this out.
2. Lack of short practical decision aids (one-page checklists or prioritization trees) for surprise events such as
unexpected bleeding — Sebastian’s primary ask.
3. Simulation fidelity and stress-exposure gap: simulations often do not recreate overlapping team dynamics and
time pressure (Jacob + Sebastian).
4. Escalation threshold uncertainty for novice staff — Simone highlighted when to call for help vs act.
