1. Ethics
– a very brief overview
Normative ethics: Theories that attempt to state the general criteria for right and
wrong actions
Assumed to make our (: everyone’s!) implicit ethics explicit (and systematic and coherent)
Two dominant theories:
Utilitarianism : An action is right if and only if it maximizes
(popular form of consequentialism)
wellbeing
Deontology (“Ethics of duty”): An action is right if and only if conforms with basic ethic
principles. The concern for maximizing wellbeing can be overruled by more
fundamental concerns: not harming innocent people, respecting and supporting
autonomy, dignity, property rights etc.

---

Ethics in practice
• The choice between utilitarianism and deontology is (often) not
very important in practice. The two theories converge (support
many of the same specific judgments)
In most cases, maximizing wellbeing entails not harming innocent people,
supporting autonomy, avoiding deception etc. – and vice versa.
The concern for wellbeing is always one important concern
(but perhaps among
others)
→ Applied ethics: Assessing specific practices (domains): medicine,
sports, journalism etc. – and technology, still more specifically e.g.
robotics)

---

Approaches to technology ethics
• Classical applied ethics: Applying utilitarianism and/or
deontology directly to cases of technology development
(e.g. will the
device lead to more wellbeing overall?) (“Top-down”)
Criticism: Ethical assessment should be more sensitive to the specific
domain in question (e.g. technology; robotics)
• Bottom-up applied ethics
(Beauchamp, 1984) (working back and forth between
application and theory; allowing the theory to be twisted)
• “Ethics by design” (recommended by the EU) (“frontloaded”; ethics constrains
the design process from the start)
• Ethics in design (”Values Sensitive Design”; integrating ethics in the
(whole) design process)

EU ethical guidelines
(“Ethics by design”) (for AI and
robotics)*
There are six general ethical principles that anyAI system must preserve and protect based on fundamental rights as enshrined in
the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (EU Charter), and in relevant international human rights law:
1. Respect for Human Agency: human beings must be respected to make their own decisions and carry out their own actions.
Respect for human agency encapsulates three more specific principles, which define fundamental human rights: autonomy,
dignityand freedom.
2. Privacy and Data governance: people have the right to privacy and data protection and these should be respected at all times;
3. Fairness: people should be given equal rights and opportunities and should not be advantaged or disadvantaged undeservedly;
4. Individual, Social and Environmental Well-being: AI systems should contribute to, and not harm, individual, social and
environmental wellbeing;
5. Transparency: the purpose, inputs and operations of AI programs should be knowable and understandable to its stakeholders;
6. Accountability and Oversight: humans should be able to understand, supervise and control the design and operation of AI
based systems, and the actors involved in their development or operation should take responsibility for the way that these
applications function and for the resulting consequences.
*https://ec.europa.eu/info/funding-tenders/opportunities/docs/2021-2027/horizon/guidance/ethics-by-design-and-ethics-of-use-approaches-for-artificial-
intelligence_he_en.pdf

The EU guidelines – elaborations, but also
new questions
(and an important observation)
• Autonomy: Respecting autonomy means allowing people to think for themselves, decide for
themselves what is right and wrong, and choose how they want to live their lives. It is important to
note that AI systems can restrict human autonomy without doing anything - simply by not catering
for the full range of human variation in lifestyle, values, beliefs and other aspects of our lives which
make us unique. Hence, such restrictions may arise without any malevolent intent, but solely as a
result of a lack of understanding about peoples’ lives, beliefs, values, and preferences. This is a
particular problem with personalisation services which do not take into consideration varying cultural
norms. In addition, personalisation services, even when taking into consideration varying cultural
norms, might restrict the information and options provided. AI based systems should avoid
restricting unreasonably individual decision-making context (see also below under “freedom”).
• Dignity: Dignity entails that every human being possesses an intrinsic worth which should never be
compromised. Hence, people should not be instrumentalized, objectified or dehumanized, but must
be treated with respect at all times, including when using or being subjected to AI-based systems.
• Freedom: Respecting freedom requires that people are not constrained in taking those actions
which they should be able to pursue as autonomous persons, such as freedom of movement,
freedom of speech, freedom of access to information, and freedom of assembly. In addition,
freedom requires the absence of constraints which undermine peoples’ autonomy, such as
coercion, deception, exploitation of vulnerabilities, and manipulation.

Limitations of classical technology ethics
• Evaluation done before (”frontloading”) or after the design process.
• Difficult to determine in advance which ethical concerns will turn out
to be relevant
• Costly and unrealistic to discard or substantially modify an otherwise
successful technological device once it has been constructed
• Tendency to focus on “big” issues (large security risks, violation of
human rights etc. Real-world ethical issues are often more mundane
(not violation of rights or danger to human lives, but risks of causing
inconvenience, disturbance, making people uncomfortable, worried
etc.
• Tendency to focus one-sidedly on risks and prohibitions. Ethics can and
should also be about making things better. (Technology can strengthen
wellbeing, autonomy, dignity etc.)

Values and experiences in human-robot
interaction
(etc.)
• Trust (mediated (enhanced) by reliability perception (as is trust in humans). Distrust
can stem solely from perceptions of novelty and strangeness, but is enhanced by
unrealiability perceptions (→ importance of not exposing users to premature,
unreliable design)
• Proximity can foster acceptance (getting accustomed to the robot) but also anxiety
and inconvenience (safety issues) (collision avoidance)
• Working with robots (or their mere presence) can cause anxiety, increased mental
strain and perceived higher workload. Typical situation: The human as supervisor
(monitoring; compensating for the unexpected). Difficult situations being left to
humans to deal with; lack of perceived support or empathy). Being in the way
without actually being in the way

Further psychological factors: Learning from
behavioral science
• People are motivated by experienced wellbeing and
convenience (and perceptions of potential immediate
changes to their wellbeing)
• Behavior change requires repetition, reward, and a
supportive environment (Wood, 2020)
• Social support, imitation and peer pressure (Frank,
2020) (the power of behavioral contagion; acceptance
thresholds and acceptance cascades)

---

How to work with perception and values?
1) Identify stakeholders. Who will (or can) be affected by the device?
(Snowball sampling: Use known stakeholders to identify further stakeholders)
2) Elicit and record stakeholder perceptions
3) Balance and interpret stakeholder perceptions with ethical
theory/ethical principles
Eliciting perceptions: Individual interviews / focus group interviews
Using iteration: Present still more specific solutions to stakeholders
Usually semi-structured interviews: Prepared questions, mentioning
particular focus points (potential concerns) – but open to where the
conversation leads)

---

Balancing reported perceptions with other
concerns
• Use reported perceptions and concerns as a rough guide
• But correct and complement them, based on
- Ethical theory and ethical guidelines (stakeholders may be ignorant of, or just not
report, important concerns)
- Knowledge of potential biases or misapprehension (a fine balance: “unfounded
worries” may have to be taken seriously; but some worries are not practically
relevant)
Knowledge that humans are prone to adapt and modify their values
The strength of in-depth interviews and focus groups (as opposed to
surveys): Possible to negotiate and refine (“calibrate”) answers
The strength of complementary observations: People don’t always
notice their own reactions
